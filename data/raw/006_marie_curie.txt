Marie Curie was a Polish-born physicist and chemist who conducted pioneering research on radioactivity, a term she herself coined. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences, and remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields: Physics in 1903, shared with her husband Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel, and Chemistry in 1911, awarded solely to her for the discovery of the elements polonium and radium. During World War I, she developed mobile radiography units, nicknamed "petites Curies," to provide X-ray services to field hospitals near the front lines. She died in 1934 from aplastic anemia, a condition almost certainly caused by her prolonged exposure to radiation during her research, much of which was conducted before the health risks of radioactivity were understood.
